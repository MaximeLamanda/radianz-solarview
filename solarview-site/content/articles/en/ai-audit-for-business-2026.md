## What is an AI audit for business?

An AI audit for business is a short diagnostic of your processes, data, and systems to find where artificial intelligence creates measurable value. Not a market study. Not a tech watch. A prioritization exercise: which use cases hold up in your environment, at roughly what cost, with what impact on time or margin.

At RADIANZ, we run it in one week. We map possible uses, estimate feasibility and ROI, then deliver a roadmap. You know what to launch now, what to postpone, and what to ignore. The full offer is on the [AI audit service page](/en/services/audit-ia).

If your "audit" is a list of tools to try, it is not an audit. It is a wishlist.

## The real problem is not a lack of ideas

Most founders and ops leaders we meet do not lack AI ideas. They have too many. Support chatbot, contract summarization, lead scoring, CRM agent, invoice extraction, sales copilot. Every week brings another demo or article promising an "immediate" win.

The hidden cost is noise. A product team starting three PoCs in parallel with no kill criteria. A CIO buying SaaS before checking whether the data is reachable. A CEO waiting for magical ROI on a process that is still poorly documented.

The audit is there to cut. You leave with a short, numbered list and a sequence. Everything else stays in the backlog, clearly labeled as such.

## Who this exercise is for

An AI audit fits people who must allocate budget before investing:

- leaders who want an action plan, not an 80-slide study
- overloaded ops teams that must choose what to automate first
- SMEs and mid-market firms with data but no clear AI roadmap
- product teams that want to validate a case before funding a build

If you already know the vendor to build a specific agent, and the case is framed (data available, stable process, clear KPI), you may not need a full audit. A sprint scoping call is enough. If several departments push AI requests at once, the audit keeps the budget from scattering.

## One-week method: four steps

Here is the flow we use. It stays deliberately simple. Complexity comes from your stack, not from the framework.

### 1. Discovery workshop

We frame your processes, data, and constraints. Goal: understand the ground truth, not fill a generic questionnaire. Who does what today? Where work stalls (copy-paste, manual validation, hunting for information)? Which apps are actually used?

We leave that workshop with a map of the business flow, not a list of buzzwords.

### 2. AI use-case mapping

We list possible uses on your real systems. We drop the ones that sound good but do not hold: no API, data too dirty, volume too low to justify the effort, every file still needing a human expert.

A surviving case has three traits: a visible business friction, reachable data (even imperfect), and an outcome a human can verify in a few minutes.

### 3. Analysis and prioritization

Each opportunity is read on three axes: effort, feasibility, impact. We keep what moves margin or frees time on a frequent process. A flashy win once a quarter loses to a medium daily win.

We also flag risks: hallucination on legal documents, lock-in to a proprietary model, need for a human in the loop. An AI case without guardrails is not a quick win. It is debt.

### 4. Roadmap and report

You leave with a quick-wins roadmap and a usable report. Ready to decide alone, or to start building with us. No tunnel: deliverables land at the end of the week, not after three months of "scoping phase."

## Concrete deliverables (what you should demand)

If someone offers an audit without a deliverables list, negotiate or walk away. Here is what ours includes, aligned with the [AI audit service page](/en/services/audit-ia):

- discovery workshop (notes and decisions)
- map of AI cases kept and dropped, with reasons
- opportunity analysis (effort / feasibility / impact)
- quick-wins roadmap (implementation order)
- usable audit report (not a decorative PDF)
- fast delivery: one week, not a quarter

"Usable" means specific: a non-technical decision-maker can open the report and know what to do on Monday. A tech lead can open the same section and see system constraints. If only the consultants who wrote it understand the document, the deliverable failed.

## Reading ROI without lying to yourself

ROI for an enterprise AI project is rarely exact to the cent in week one. What you can do is bound the reasoning.

For an automation case, start from volume: how many occurrences per month, how many human minutes today, what fully loaded cost. Multiply. Subtract remaining human review time (often 10 to 30% depending on risk). You get an annual savings range. Compare it to build + run cost (APIs, hosting, maintenance, supervision).

For a decision-support case (scoring, prioritization, recommendation), measure today's error rate or delay, then estimate the impact of a partial improvement. A sales scoring model that cuts 20% of time wasted on C leads does not "transform" anything. It can still pay for the project in six months.

Three common traps:

1. Counting 100% of human time as recovered when review still remains.
2. Ignoring data prep cost (cleaning, access rights, pipelines).
3. Comparing ROI of a throwaway PoC to ROI of a production tool: they are not the same object.

A serious audit also says "no": cases too rare, data unreachable, negative ROI outside an optimistic scenario. That "no" is often worth more than an enthusiastic "yes."

## What an audit is not

It is not an inventory of LLM brands. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or an open-source model are just building blocks. Model choice comes after the use case, not before.

It is also not an eighteen-month digital transformation program. If someone promises an "AI roadmap to 2030" with no quick wins in 30 to 90 days, you are buying prose.

Finally, it is not an excuse to delay construction. For us, the audit opens the door to production. If the case is clear, we move on to an agent, an automation, or a platform. If you prefer to build in-house, you keep the plan. Both outcomes are fine.

## How to start without burning a week of calendar

Before the workshop, prepare three things. A draft list of candidate processes. Read access to the systems involved, or at least one business contact and one tech contact. A success criterion for leadership: time saved, errors avoided, margin, cycle time.

Then [book a discovery call](/en/contact). Fifteen minutes is enough to see whether the one-week format fits. No catalog pitch. If it is the wrong moment, we say so.

A well-run AI audit for business does not produce more ideas. It removes some. What remains fits on a page you can defend in a steering committee.
