## Why scoring matters

Most teams lose time on opportunities that look good but fail during technical review. A warehouse with an impressive footprint can still be a dead end once you account for roof pitch, HVAC equipment, or consumption that does not support self-consumption.

A shared scoring model creates alignment between marketing, sales, and engineering. Everyone works from the same definition of "priority" — which means fewer arguments in pipeline reviews and faster decisions on which sites get a site visit.

Scoring is not about replacing judgment. It is about front-loading the obvious filters so reps spend their limited hours on deals that have a realistic path to close.

## Signals to include in your score

Use a blend of roof geometry, local irradiance context, and business fit criteria. At minimum, track these four groups.

**Roof geometry:** usable area after deducting setbacks and obstructions, approximate pitch, and visible shading sources (adjacent buildings, equipment). **Irradiance context:** annual production potential for the site location — two roofs of equal size in different regions score differently. **Business fit:** sector code, estimated consumption profile, distance from your install base, and compatibility with your commercial model (self-consumption, PPA, direct sale). **Deal velocity:** regulatory hook (solarization mandate), known decision-maker access, and competitive activity on the account.

Keep the model explainable so account executives can justify prioritization decisions to prospects and to their own managers. A black-box score that nobody can defend in a meeting will be ignored within a month.

Weight signals based on what actually predicts conversion in your pipeline — not on what sounds sophisticated. If your best-closing deals are all logistics warehouses above 5,000 m², that pattern should show up in your weights.

## Building a repeatable execution loop

Turn scoring output into daily actions: shortlist, outreach, follow-up, and status tracking. A score without a next step is just a label.

Define three tiers — A, B, and C — with explicit rules for each. A-tier sites get contacted within 48 hours with a prepared proposal. B-tier sites enter a nurture sequence or batch outreach. C-tier sites stay in the database but do not consume rep time until something changes (new consumption data, renovation permit, regulatory deadline).

Review conversion by score bracket each month to improve your model over time. If B-tier sites close at the same rate as A-tier, your thresholds are too conservative. If A-tier sites rarely convert, you are overweighting the wrong signals.

Scoring works best as part of a broader [B2B solar prospecting](/articles/b2b-solar-prospecting-2026) workflow that combines data filtering with field validation. The score gets you to the right door; the visit confirms whether to knock.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not score on total building footprint alone — cadastral area overstates usable roof by 30–50% on many commercial sites. Do not treat all sectors equally: a retail hypermarket and a small office park need different consumption assumptions. Do not let scores go stale: a building that was C-tier six months ago may be A-tier after a renovation or mandate update.

Finally, resist the urge to add too many criteria early on. Start with five to seven signals, run the model for one sales cycle, then refine. A simple score used consistently beats a complex score nobody maintains.
